Chapter 4

Length is the enemy of a strut

Your guess first

Take a steel bar that holds when it is 1 m long. Make it 8 m long and push on it. How much of that strength is left?

Pulling is easy

A 100 mm steel box section can hold in tension. Make it 1 m long or 8 m long — it doesn't matter. Tension just stretches the steel until it yields.

Pushing is different

Squeeze the same bar and it fails sideways — it buckles. At 1 m it carries ; at 4 m only ; at 8 m just .

Euler showed in 1757 that buckling strength falls with the square of the length. Timber, far less stiff than steel, suffers even sooner.

The design rule

Keep compression members short and stocky; let long members work in tension. That's why bridges are full of triangles with short top chords — and why cables, which can only pull, can be so long and thin.

Field note · 1907
The Québec Bridge

On 29 August 1907 the partly built Québec Bridge collapsed into the St. Lawrence River, killing 75 workers. The investigation found that compression chords near the main pier had buckled: the bridge was heavier than its designers had assumed, and those members could not carry it. The rebuilt bridge still holds the record for the longest cantilever truss span — 549 m.

Field note · 1940
Tacoma Narrows

The first Tacoma Narrows Bridge had an 853 m main span stiffened by a girder only about 2.4 m deep — extremely slender. Four months after opening, a steady wind of around 64 km/h set the deck twisting until it tore apart. Nobody died, and every long-span bridge since has been checked for stiffness and wind, not just strength.

Chapter 7

Pick the right shape for the gap

Every form has a sweet spot

Beams are simple but run out of strength quickly. Trusses use triangles to go further. Arches turn load into pure compression. Cable-stayed and suspension bridges hang the deck from cables, which only pull — so they span the farthest.

Where you start

In the Span simulator you'll build from 10 m to 48 m — the territory of trusses, small arches and short cable bridges. The physics is the same all the way up to two-kilometre suspension spans.

How these numbers were made

The bridge is a 16 m steel Warren truss (8 deck panels of 2 m, 2.5 m deep; SHS 150×8 diagonals and SHS 200×10 top chord) carrying a 26 t, three-axle tipper. Static forces use a linear-elastic direct-stiffness truss solver; buckling uses the Rankine–Gordon combination of squash load and Euler load; design checks use 1.35·G + 1.5·Q with γM = 1.0 (steel) and 1.25 (timber). The load test in chapter 6 is the simulator's dynamic XPBD model (40 substeps per frame) on a timber variant. Span ranges in chapter 7 are approximate typical economic ranges; records are well-known examples and newer bridges may exceed them.
